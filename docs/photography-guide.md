# Shooting product photos for the site

A short, repeatable setup. Follow it and a new knife drops straight into the
site with no retouching; skip it and the photo needs work every time.

## Before you press the shutter

**Shoot RAW,** or if the camera or phone cannot, maximum-quality JPEG /
Apple ProRAW. Everything on the site is derived from the original, so the
original has to hold the detail.

**Fill the frame.** The knife should occupy most of the picture with a hand's
width of space around it. Every current photo wastes half its pixels on empty
background, which is the single biggest reason the catalogue looks soft.

**Long side 3000px minimum.** Today's files are 900×1200. That is the ceiling on
how good the site can look, and nothing downstream can raise it.

**One knife per photo.** A group shot cannot be used on a product page — it has
to be cropped down to one knife, which throws away resolution and sometimes
clips a neighbour into frame. Shoot the group *as well*, for the series page,
but shoot each knife alone first.

## The setup

**Tripod, always.** Even in good light. It is the difference between a sharp
edge and a nearly-sharp one, and it lets you shoot the same framing twice.

**Diffused light, from the side.** An overcast day, open shade, or a window
with a sheer curtain. Raking light across the blade is what shows hammer marks,
grind lines and handle grain. Direct sun blows out the steel and kills all
three.

**Neutral backdrop.** Matte grey, weathered wood or dark stone. Nothing
patterned, nothing coloured — a coloured surface bounces onto the blade and
shifts the steel's tone. Keep the backdrop consistent across a run so the
products sit together on a grid.

**Include a colour reference** in one frame per session — a grey card, or even
a sheet of white paper — so the whole set can be corrected to the same
neutral later.

## The frames to take

For each knife:

1. **Profile,** blade horizontal, filling the frame. This is the card image.
2. **Three-quarter,** angled so the handle's depth and the sheath read.
3. **Handle detail,** close, showing grain, pins, bolster and any spacer.
4. **Blade detail,** showing the grind, the finish and any maker's mark.
5. **With the sheath,** if one ships with it.
6. **In a hand,** for scale. One frame is enough and it settles every "how big
   is it really" question.

Keep the whole blade in focus — stop down to f/8 or f/11 if you have the
control. A shallow depth of field looks good on a portrait and hides the
product on a knife.

## Don't

- Don't send anything through Instagram, WhatsApp or Messages. Every one of
  them recompresses, and the damage cannot be undone. AirDrop, a cable, or
  email as "actual size".
- Don't sharpen, brighten or filter before sending. The site does its own
  processing from the original.
- Don't crop tightly. Leave room around the silhouette — the site crops
  automatically and needs margin to work with.
- Don't shoot on a coloured towel, a rug or a bedspread.
